Introduction to Synonyms
Vocabulary | Synonyms
Hook
[VISUAL: Timeline animates fast — Anglo-Saxon → French (1066) → Latin/Greek layers stacking into modern English] My dear students — here's a fact most people never learn. English absorbed vocabulary from multiple invasions and influences over centuries. Each time, instead of replacing old words, English kept both.
That's why 'ask,' 'question,' and 'interrogate' all exist for one idea, at three different levels of formality.
That's not a coincidence. That's the reason English has so many synonyms. Let's understand how to use that to your advantage.

What is it?
[VISUAL: "SYNONYM" splits into "SYN" (together) + "ONYM" (name)] A synonym is a word that means nearly the same as another word. Not identical. Nearly. Hold onto that word — the entire lesson lives inside it.

The Analogy
[VISUAL: A wardrobe opens. Inside: five shirts, all technically "blue," but each a different shade — navy, sky, teal, cobalt, denim] Think of synonyms like this wardrobe. Every shirt is blue. But you wouldn't wear the same blue to a wedding and a workout. Synonyms work the same way — same core meaning, different shade, different occasion. Choosing the right one isn't decoration. It's precision.

Why is it important?
[VISUAL: Two panels side by side — "A written email" and "A spoken conversation"] This precision matters far beyond any exam. A manager who writes 'I'm concerned' sends a different message than one who writes 'I'm worried' — same core meaning, different weight. A lawyer choosing 'negligent' over 'careless' is making a specific legal point. This is a skill used in every email, every conversation, every report.

Rules / Core Concepts
[VISUAL: Venn diagram — two circles overlapping mostly, slivers not touching] One — synonyms overlap in meaning but differ in intensity or formality. 'Big' and 'enormous' both mean large. Enormous is louder.
Two — context decides which synonym is right. The correct 'shirt' depends on the 'occasion' — the sentence around it.

Three — most advanced English words come from Latin or Greek roots. Learn one root, unlock five words.

Examples
[VISUAL: Three real scenes]
Candid → Frank [a direct, honest conversation between colleagues] Meticulous → Careful [someone reviewing a document line by line] Ephemeral → Fleeting [a market trend rising and disappearing within weeks] Candid and frank both mean honest — but candid carries an unfiltered, sometimes blunt edge. That shade is the whole game.

Common Mistakes
[VISUAL: A word chosen for sounding similar gets flagged with a soft red outline]
The single biggest mistake: confusing words related in topic with words close in meaning. 'Famous' and 'popular' get mixed up constantly. Famous means widely known. Popular means widely liked. A person can be famous without being popular at all.

The Process — step by step
[VISUAL: A four-step ladder builds on screen, one rung at a time] Step one — Isolate. Take the word completely out of its sentence.
Step two — Define in your own words. If you can't define it simply, you don't know it yet.
Step three — Predict before you peek. Come up with your own synonym before looking at any options.
Step four — Match by shade, not by sound. Pick the option closest in meaning and intensity.
